Own Rental Property? Here Are 5 Tax Strategies Worth Knowing.

A tax planning guide for rental property owners. Rental real estate can provide powerful tax benefits — but simply owning a rental doesn't guarantee you'll receive them. The difference often comes down to how you operate the property, how you structure your activities, and when you plan.

Here are five strategies rental property owners should know.

Strategy 01

Short-Term Rental Losses May Offset Your W-2 or Business Income

This is extremely powerful for W-2 earners who also have rental properties. Many new rental owners are surprised to learn that rental losses generally cannot offset W-2 wages or active business income — because most rental activities are treated as passive for tax purposes. Certain short-term rentals can be different.

A short-term rental may fall outside the passive rental-activity rules when the average guest stay is 7 days or less. The average stay is generally determined by dividing total rental days by the number of separate stays.

The second key requirement is material participation. You should carefully track your time and satisfy at least one of the material participation tests, such as:

  • Spending more than 500 hours in the activity
  • Spending more than 100 hours, and more than any other individual
  • Performing substantially all of the work

If the average guest stay is 7 days or less and you materially participate, the activity may be treated as nonpassive — meaning qualifying losses, including depreciation, may potentially offset W-2 wages, active business income, and other non-passive income.

Bonus: Combine Short-Term Rental + Cost Segregation
Pairing a short-term rental with cost segregation can significantly accelerate depreciation and potentially create a deductible tax loss.

Example: A rental generates $10,000 of income before depreciation.

Without cost segregation:

  • Regular depreciation: $6,000
  • Taxable rental income: $4,000

With cost segregation:

  • Approximately $50,000 of the property is reclassified into qualifying shorter-life assets eligible for 100% bonus depreciation — deductible immediately.

The rental could go from $4,000 of taxable income to approximately a ($40,000) tax loss. If the short-term rental rules are met and you materially participate, that loss may potentially offset W-2 or other non-passive income.

Important: Before purchasing or converting a property to a short-term rental, check local city, county, HOA, and licensing requirements. Some jurisdictions restrict short-term rentals to primary residences or prohibit them altogether.

Strategy 02

Real Estate Professional Status Could Unlock Rental Losses

For most investors, rental losses are considered passive and may be suspended if there isn't enough passive income to absorb them. However, taxpayers who qualify as a Real Estate Professional and materially participate in their rental activities may be able to treat those losses as nonpassive — potentially offsetting W-2 wages, active business income, and other nonpassive income.

To qualify as a Real Estate Professional, you generally must meet both of these tests:

  • Spend more than 750 hours during the year in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate; and
  • Spend more than 50% of your total personal-service time during the year in those real property trades or businesses.

Qualifying real property trades or businesses include development, redevelopment, construction, reconstruction, acquisition, conversion, rental, operation, management, leasing, and brokerage.

For married couples: Only one spouse needs to independently meet the Real Estate Professional tests. If material participation requirements are also met, qualifying rental losses may be treated as nonpassive on the joint return.

Planning Opportunity: Don't wait until tax season to determine whether you qualify. Your hours, activities, and supporting documentation should be tracked and evaluated throughout the year.
Strategy 03

Active Participation: Deduct Up to $25,000 of Rental Losses

You don't necessarily need to qualify as a Real Estate Professional to deduct rental losses against other income. If you actively participate in your rental real estate activity, you may qualify for a special allowance permitting up to $25,000 of rental losses to offset nonpassive income.

Active participation generally means you're involved in meaningful management decisions, such as:

  • Approving tenants
  • Setting rental terms
  • Authorizing repairs and expenditures
  • Making other significant management decisions

Income limitation: The $25,000 allowance generally begins to phase out when modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) exceeds $100,000, and is generally fully phased out at $150,000, subject to applicable rules and exceptions.

Planning Opportunity: If your tax return shows suspended rental losses, review whether you qualify for the $25,000 special allowance and whether changes in your income or circumstances could allow you to use them.
Strategy 04

Cost Segregation Could Accelerate Your Depreciation Deduction

A residential rental building is generally depreciated over 27.5 years — but not every component of the property necessarily has to follow the same schedule. A cost segregation study can identify qualifying components — appliances, flooring, cabinetry, landscaping, fencing, and other improvements — that may be depreciated over 5, 7, or 15 years instead.

This can significantly accelerate depreciation into the early years of ownership. Under current federal law, certain qualifying shorter-life assets may be eligible for 100% bonus depreciation, allowing their cost to be deducted in the year placed in service.

For the right property, cost segregation combined with bonus depreciation could potentially generate $100,000 or more in first-year depreciation deductions.

Cost segregation may be worth considering if you:

  • Recently purchased a rental property
  • Purchased a higher-value property
  • Completed a significant renovation
  • Can currently benefit from the additional depreciation deductions
Planning Opportunity: Generating a large deduction doesn't necessarily mean you can use it immediately. Cost segregation should be evaluated together with the passive activity loss rules to determine whether the additional depreciation will actually reduce your current taxable income.
Strategy 05

Selling a Rental with a Large Gain? A 1031 Exchange Could Potentially Defer the Tax

A successful rental investment can create another tax problem: a large taxable gain when you sell. For example, suppose you purchased a rental years ago and now expect a gain of more than $200,000. A sale may result in federal and state income taxes, as well as tax consequences associated with depreciation previously claimed.

A properly structured Section 1031 exchange may allow you to defer qualifying gain by exchanging the property for other qualifying investment real estate.

Sell → Pay the Tax → Reinvest What's Left

Instead, you may be able to:

Sell → Defer the Tax → Keep More Capital Invested

Timing matters: replacement property must be identified within 45 days, and the exchange must be completed within 180 days, subject to applicable requirements.

Planning Opportunity: Talk with your tax advisor and qualified intermediary before your sale closes. Waiting until after receiving the proceeds may be too late.
